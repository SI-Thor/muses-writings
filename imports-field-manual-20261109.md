## 0. The spine — one economy, six parts

Six things arrived in one morning: a speech model the size of a song file, a library of six thousand licensed 3D props, two papers about *structure* (one about code as a draft for a renderer, one about handing work down a capability ladder), one paper about *memory* (turning what an agent did into an asset it keeps), and a tiny vision model whose vendor sells the whole arrangement as a product line. They are not six separate curiosities. Together they describe one economy, and the economy is the thing to hold:

**Expensive minds belong at the joints. Cheap machinery belongs in the long runs. What passes between them is structure — a draft, a scenario, a gene, a baton — never raw bulk. And the last mile, where the work meets a human, should be rendered by models so small they live on the box the human is already looking at.**

The six parts, one line each:

1. **Code is the draft.** A short program can state counts, layout, motion and timing exactly; a generative renderer cannot. Video-Coder's measurement (full-text, §1.1): putting a code-built white-model draft in front of a strong video generator raised its physics-consistency average from 0.61 to 0.67 and its VBench-2.0 physics plausibility by ~24 points. The draft carries the structure; the expensive surface carries only the surface.
2. **Capability hands DOWN, at a stage boundary, once.** AgentBaton's measurement (full-text, §1.2): the capable model opens, builds the substantive artefact and gets real feedback; the efficient model finishes. Cost falls 47–86% depending on the pair; accuracy holds or improves. The reverse direction — cheap first, expensive rescue — loses on both axes. A small *detector*, not the working model, calls the moment.
3. **Experience compiles into assets.** Evolver's measurement (full-text, §1.3): procedural knowledge extracted from past runs into structured Gene objects, retrieved into later runs, cut token consumption by 24.24% on a selected diagnostic set and raised success by 4.38 points — no weight updates, no retraining. Yesterday's exploration becomes tomorrow's starting capital.
4. **Tiny specialists render the last mile.** Woof (card, §1.4): 40.6 MB, both voices, pure numpy, one CPU thread — claimed 1.42% word-error rate on a hard-case bench, better than models a thousand times its size, because it specialises in the register that matters (numbers, dates, codes said aloud). The last mile does not need a brain; it needs a voice that never misreads a phone number.
5. **Licensed props sit on the shelf.** Objaverse-clean-GLB (card, §1.5): 6,015 models, CC0/CC-BY only, quality-ranked, unit-scaled, load-tested, with per-model credit carried in the metadata. World-building stops meaning "generate everything from scratch" and starts meaning "cast from a catalogue, generate the gaps."
6. **Perception can be tiny too — and routers are products now.** Mirage (thin card + vendor, §1.6): a small vision model returning *structure* (detections, OCR, spatial relations) rather than prose, sold alongside a confidence-scored router that decides per answer whether the device suffices or the cloud is called. That router is part 2's detector, commercialised: the economy is no longer just a paper pattern, it is a shop pattern.

**The sentence to hold:** what this economy buys us that buying a bigger model doesn't is *placement*. A bigger model makes every step smarter at the same price per step, forever. This economy makes the expensive steps rare and structural, the cheap steps numerous and safe, and the knowledge compound — a scenario, a gene, a prop, a voice — so that next month's run starts from this month's assets instead of from zero. Our own doctrines were already this shape (compile the loop, digest the exceptions; the citizens' compilation curve; MiniMax-first execution). What the morning's imports add is not the shape. It is measurements of the shape, taken by strangers, on their own benches — plus two physical assets (a voice, a prop shelf) we can hold today.

### 1.1 Video-Coder — code drafts for generative renderers

**Evidence: FULL-TEXT** (arXiv:2611.09894v1, read entire). Base model Qwen3.5-9B; downstream generator MiniMax-H3; paper CC BY 4.0; data, models and code stated open-sourced (HF: Video-Coder). One correction to the first-pass note: §4.1 of the paper says SFT on "Video-Coder-SFT-10k" and RL on "Video-Coder-RL-4k," while §3.1 constructs 6k and 2k datasets of those names. The paper contradicts itself on dataset sizes; the manual carries both figures with the discrepancy named, and treats 6k/2k as the constructed sets because §3.1 is the construction account.

**What it is.** A trained coding agent that, given a text prompt, writes and iteratively revises Blender Python to build a dynamic 3D scene, renders it as an untextured grey "white-model" draft video, and hands that draft — structure and motion only — to a diffusion video generator, which adds appearance. The agent is trained, not just prompted: SFT on synthetic agentic trajectories, then GRPO reinforcement learning inside a purpose-built harness, with two rewards annealed against each other.

**The mechanism.** The harness is the load-bearing piece, and it is recognisably a cousin of our own rigs. Each episode runs in an isolated sandbox with Blender installed; every scene renders under one unified setting (grey clay, fixed resolution and frame rate) so the draft carries *only* structure and motion. The agent works through two tools — `check_script` (fast static check) and `run_blender` (execute + render) — and after each call the harness returns three feedback types: execution feedback (syntax errors, missing cameras, tracebacks; on failure this is the *only* feedback), content checks (format verified; the camera's actual view inspected — empty frames, static video, subject out of frame — with a brief scene summary), and visual feedback (evenly spaced frames as images). A submission is not trusted: the harness re-runs the final script from scratch in a clean directory and accepts the episode only if the independent re-execution passes all checks. That acceptance rule — *verify by re-execution in a store the author cannot write* — is the Watcher's Charter wearing a lab coat, and it is worth naming because it is the strongest single design decision in the paper.

The data pipeline is four stages: prompts collected from public video-generation datasets and rewritten by an LLM into scene-centric form (objects + counts, layout, motion, camera — appearance-heavy prompts discarded as unbuildable); trajectories generated by connecting Gemini 3.8 Flash to the harness and letting it iterate to a matching draft or the turn limit; ground-truth videos synthesised by feeding prompt + draft into MiniMax-H3; then three-level filtering (execution validity; white-model quality; prompt–video consistency judged by Seed2.0, sub-threshold samples removed). SFT-6k = prompt + full trajectory + reference video; RL-2k = prompt–reference pairs (see the size discrepancy above).

Rewards, exactly. Execution reward `R_exe` ∈ {0, 1}: 1 iff all tool-call formats are correct *and* the final program executes *and* passes all environment checks. Video reward `R_video` ∈ [0, 1]: a model judge (Qwen3.8-omni-flash, prompt in the paper's Appendix B) scoring the final video's consistency with prompt and reference across object categories, spatial layout, motion dynamics and camera behaviour. Final reward: **R = α·R_video + e^(−steps/T)·R_exe, α = 0.5** — the execution term decays exponentially over training while the video term holds constant, so early training is dominated by "make it run" and late training by "make it good." Optimisation is GRPO, group size 8, up to 100 interaction turns, 120K context; training took ~3 days on 8 H100s for the agent plus 16 H100s serving H3 rollouts.

Results (author-measured, full-text). PhyGenBench (GPT-4o-judged physics consistency, [0,1]): Video-Coder-9B + MiniMax-H3 averages **0.67** vs H3 alone **0.61** — the abstract's "approximately 7 points" is 6 points by the tables. VBench-2.0 physics dimensions: 78.19% average vs H3's 54.58% (+23.61 points; mechanics +21.08, thermotics +23.66, material +26.08) — but note CogVideoX-1.5 sits at 77.04% *without* any draft, and beats the pair on material (83.19% vs 75.02%). Transfer: the same agent in front of OmniWeaving lifts it 0.48 → 0.51 (the results text calls this "an increase of 16.28 points," an arithmetic error in the paper — the tables give +0.03). The ablation (PhyGenBench average) is the honest core: H3 alone 0.61 → SFT-only agent 0.63 → full model 0.67; removing the video reward 0.64; removing the execution reward 0.63; fixed-weight rewards instead of annealing 0.65. Read plainly: **the untrained-pipeline contribution (a code draft at all, learned by imitation) is worth +2; the RL is worth a further +4; each reward and the annealing are each worth +2–4.** The draft idea does not need their training budget to be worth something.

### 1.2 AgentBaton — capability handed down, once, at the right moment

**Evidence: FULL-TEXT** (arXiv:2610.35959v1, read entire). Authors HKUST + NVIDIA-affiliated leads; scaffold mini-SWE-agent v2.4.0; paper CC BY 4.0.

**What it is.** A study of *within-task* model collaboration: one model starts a long agentic task, another finishes it. The study's findings reduce to three: direction decides (capable→efficient works, efficient→capable doesn't), one handoff is enough, and the moment to hand off is recognisable — recognisable enough that a 2B detector, fine-tuned for the purpose, beats both fixed thresholds and per-turn routing.

**The mechanism.** Setup: capable models GPT-5.6 Sol, Kimi K3, GLM-5.3 (reasoning at max); efficient model Qwen3.8-27B (xhigh) — explicitly chosen as strong-for-its-size and *deployable on local workstations*. Benchmarks: Terminal-Bench 2.1 and DeepSWE v1.1; two-hour timeout, 16K new tokens per turn; API cost from official token pricing, with **locally deployable models accounted at zero cost** — a convention that flatters every configuration whose tail runs locally, and must be remembered when quoting the savings. Context transfer comes in two forms, and the difference matters: *in-context* (the full interaction history goes into the receiver's context) vs *logfile* (a structured execution history written to a file the receiver inspects on demand with ordinary commands). The receiver always inherits the workspace itself.

Direction and transfer, with numbers (full-text). Efficient→capable: even delaying the capable model's arrival, savings are thin and accuracy inconsistent — at 30% progress, handing *up* to GPT-5.6 Sol gives 70.80% vs 68.10% for Sol alone at only **6.1% lower cost**; for GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3, later up-handoffs *lose* accuracy as they save. Logfile transfer is the better form in this direction (matches or beats in-context accuracy at significantly lower cost in most cases). Capable→efficient: at the right stage, accuracy holds at a fraction of the cost — Kimi K3 handing off at 45% progress: **72.57% vs 73.45% capable-only, cost −61.5%**; GPT-5.6 Sol via logfile at 60%: 67.26% vs 68.10%, cost −47.9%; GLM-5.3 at 30%: 61.1%, *matching* its baseline, cost **−82.4%**. Transfer preference in this direction is model-dependent: in-context for GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3, mixed for Sol — the authors hypothesise because Sol does not expose its full chain-of-thought, so in-context transfer carries less of its reasoning.

Readiness, the paper's most portable finding (§3.3): comparing trajectories where the efficient model fails after an early handoff but succeeds after a later one, three recurring signs mark a handoff-ready state — **(1) a substantive implementation, runnable prototype, or task-required artefact exists; (2) real execution/testing/inspection has produced task-relevant feedback (a failed run counts, if it narrows the problem); (3) the remaining work has become localised — diagnosis, repair, validation, finalisation.** Readiness is a property of the *state of the work*, not of elapsed turns — which is why fixed-turn thresholds underperform and why the authors' second experiment matters: asked to hand off by prompt alone, capable models mostly won't (Kimi K3 handed off on 0 of 113 tasks; GLM-5.3 on 6; only Sol complied, at an unfavourable trade). The working model cannot be trusted to call its own handoff; the call belongs to a separate, cheap judge.

The detector: fine-tuned from **Qwen 3.5 2B** (a 9B variant showed no clear improvement), trained on 4,000 trajectories from 2,000 SWE-rebench V2 tasks (two of five capable models per task: GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 5, Kimi K3, GLM-5.3), with handoff points annotated retrospectively by Codex under a written protocol (Appendix B: evidence restrictions, the three readiness criteria, earliest-suitable-point selection, a no-clear-handoff label). Supervision is per-turn binary JSON — HANDOFF_NOT_READY until the annotated turn, HANDOFF_READY at and after it. At inference it re-judges after every turn (median 338 ms, p95 1.11 s on one H100); first READY transfers control, once. Detected points land at 26.6–41.1% of trajectory on DeepSWE, 55.6–68.9% on Terminal-Bench — *the right moment is benchmark-shaped, not universal*. Headline results across 11 model pairs × 2 benchmarks: cost −65.9% on average (abstract figure); 12 of 22 (pair, benchmark) settings improve accuracy or lose <5% relative; the standout is Opus→Luna on DeepSWE, 67.3% → **76.1%** accuracy at −70.5% cost. Against alternatives: query-level routing collapses (a fine-tuned router picked the cheap model for 1 task in the whole set — effectively capable-only); per-turn routing (MTRouter) costs 46.7–86.6% *more* than AgentBaton at equal or worse accuracy; fixed-progress handoff — which cheats, using the final trajectory length unavailable online — merely ties the detector on accuracy at equal or higher cost. The reasoning-effort sweep extends the frontier at both ends (Luna→Qwen at xhigh matches Luna standalone accuracy at ~$0.06 vs $0.21 per task). Failure cases, stated by the paper's own tables: 10 of 22 settings lose ≥5% relative accuracy; Luna-as-source handing down to Qwen loses 7.3% / 5.7% relative on the two benchmarks even while saving 75.0% / 42.9% — handing down from an already-cheap start buys cost with accuracy.

### 1.3 Evolver — exploration, compiled into Gene assets

**Evidence: FULL-TEXT** (arXiv:2611.07595v1, submitted 5 Nov 2026, read entire). System: Evolver v2.0.33, open at github.com/EvoMap/evolver; EvoMap is the authors' own platform (evomap.ai) — an interest to note, since the network layer of the system is also their product.

**What it is.** A framework that turns what an agent *did* — its exploration, failures, recoveries — into persistent, structured, reusable capability, without touching model weights. Where Video-Coder trains the drafter and AgentBaton schedules the handoff, Evolver manages the *residue*: the procedural knowledge that otherwise dies inside a transcript.

**The mechanism.** The unit is the **Gene**: a structured strategy object g = (s, 𝒮, 𝒫, ℳ, 𝒱) — a summary of the experience; the procedural steps; the **applicability conditions**; task-matching signals; and an optional validation plan. The paper is explicit, and the manual repeats it because it bounds everything below: applicability conditions and validation plans are *guidance*, not enforceable constraints or proof of prior verification. Around the Gene sit three companion assets: a **Capsule** (the Gene bound to one concrete execution case — the worked example that travels with the rule), an **AntiGene** (negative guidance: what to avoid — the only one of the four that is a first-class citizen here *and* in our own lessons files), and Event/provenance/review records. Storage is content-addressed and append-only; review state lives in separate records, so an asset can be quarantined, approved, rejected or revoked *without overwriting the asset itself* — eligibility is a layer over identity, the same separation our ledger discipline arrived at independently.

The loop has four moves. **Acquisition:** harness adapters ingest session records (task context, tool interactions, errors, outcomes), normalised and heuristically redacted. **Extraction:** lightweight signals of success/failure/difficulty nominate candidates; the default pathway drafts concise procedural hints from assistant utterances and marks them **unproven**; optional LLM distillation produces fuller Genes, Capsules and AntiGenes. Drafted and distilled candidates enter **quarantine** for review. **Retrieval:** at run time, experience returns through session-start hooks (a bounded set of locally eligible Genes), prompt-time recall matched against the task, MCP tools on demand, or — in autonomous execution — a heuristic selector weighing relevance, quality and historical reuse feedback. Gene + Capsule are injected into the existing context; the harness, the model and its native verification are untouched. **Evolution:** execution feedback (outcomes, validation results, reuse events) drives the lifecycle — quarantine → approval/rejection, an optional probation mechanism promoting on recorded successes and failures, failure-based restrictions inside the selector. EvoMap adds a network layer (publish/discover/retrieve across agents) whose imported assets arrive *untrusted until promoted* — with the paper's own caveat that admission and trust enforcement are pathway-dependent and some direct retrieval interfaces do not apply the same restrictions.

The evaluation, with its fine print attached (full-text, §4.1/§4.4). Protocol B–E–G: baseline run with no injection; extraction of Genes from *valid baseline trajectories, successes and failures both*; then a fresh-session re-run with selected Genes injected (same-task Genes prioritised, then pool matches; **injection budget 12,000 bytes**; pools isolated by batch/benchmark/harness/model; assets frozen before the augmented run). Benchmarks Terminal-Bench 2.1, SWE-bench Multilingual, SWE-rebench, GAIA; harnesses Claude Code and Codex; models **deepseek-v4.1-flash** and **gpt-5.6-sol** — the first of those is, notably, our own league driver's model class. Scale: the first 20 tasks per benchmark in original order, empty-response tasks replaced sequentially, 20 valid pairs retained per configuration, 16 configurations → **320 paired observations**. Success: 83.13% → 87.50% (**+4.38 points**); by harness, Claude Code +2.50 (85.00 → 87.50), Codex +6.25 (81.25 → 87.50). By benchmark: Terminal-Bench +8.75 (64/80 → 71/80), SWE-rebench +5.00, GAIA +3.75, SWE-bench Multilingual **unchanged at 96.25%** — a ceiling case, where the interesting number is cost. Configuration spread: 9 of 16 improved, **7 unchanged, none worse**. Tokens: 1,641.1K → 1,243.3K average per task (**−24.24%** aggregate; per-configuration range −13.55% to −35.96%); among the 265 pairs successful under both conditions, 1,278.2K → 1,053.6K (**−17.57%**). Two case studies carry the shape: a token-counting task at −86.5% tokens and 112 → 17 model invocations; an RxJava repair at −58.2% and 86 → 52 invocations.

Where "selected" bites, in the authors' own words (§4.1): pairs were retained only with valid outcomes and complete token records under both conditions, executions interrupted by timeouts or token limits were excluded — **and "the final task selection additionally considers observed outcome improvements and token usage, making the resulting set diagnostic rather than a random sample of the full benchmarks."** The +4.38 is therefore an upper-bound-flavoured number on a success-tilted subset; the paper says so itself, which is to its credit and must travel with the figure. Three further limits the paper states or implies: token accounting **excludes extraction and all overhead outside the final task executions** (the distillation pass that makes the Genes is unpriced); retrieval was *same-task-first* within an isolated pool — this measures **reuse of experience about a task family you then re-attempt**, not generalisation to unseen tasks, which the authors name as future work along with longitudinal evolution; and the whole loop ran **once** (extract → re-run), so "evolution" in the evaluated system is a single generation, not a compounding lineage.

**What it helps us with.**

| Puzzle | What Evolver changes about the attack | First move (cost) | Measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| The fleet's own learning — skills, AGENTS.md lessons, seat briefs | We already run this loop by hand and by habit; Evolver gives it a *schema and a lifecycle*. The mapping is nearly one-to-one: a Gene is a lesson with applicability conditions and matching signals (our AGENTS.md entries carry the lesson but only sometimes the *when-it-applies*); a Capsule is the incident that taught it (our entries cite incidents in prose — a Capsule makes the citation structural); an AntiGene is our "never again" class, currently indistinguishable from positive lessons in the same file; quarantine is the gap we actually have — a freshly distilled lesson enters our files at full authority the moment it is written, with no probation state between "one lane noticed" and "standing rule." | Build (small): add the Gene fields to the lessons format — applicability conditions + matching signals as required fields on new AGENTS.md/skill entries, and a probation marker for lessons with a single observation behind them (build) | Lessons retrieved/applied per lane-week; probation lessons promoted vs revoked (a zero-revocation year means the lifecycle is theatre) |

**What it doesn't do / where the claim is soft.** The headline is a selected diagnostic set (above) — the honest import is the *efficiency-on-shared-success* figure (−17.57% among pairs that succeeded both ways), which survives the selection critique better because it doesn't depend on the success delta. Even that excludes extraction cost. Nothing here was measured on citizens, worlds, shows or any non-coding harness; the harnesses *are* our class of harness (Claude Code is a fleet seat) but the tasks are issue-resolution and terminal work, so transfer to a Minecraft day is a hypothesis, not a result. And a structural caution from our own record: a Gene whose applicability conditions are *guidance* can be retrieved into a situation it doesn't fit and followed fluently — our version of that failure is already named (a repair asserted is not a repair proven); probation + feedback scoring is the mitigation, and it only works if revocations actually happen.

### 1.4 Underdog Woof — a voice the size of a song file

**Evidence: CARD** (HF ConwayResearch/Underdog-Woof, read in full on arrival, 2026-11-09). All performance figures are **author-measured**; the bench is the authors' own; transcripts that produced the word-error rates were generated by qwen3.8-omni-flash and errors counted by gemma-4-31b-it — a model-judged metric, two instruments deep.

**What it is.** A small English text-to-speech model: 38.24M parameters total (22.95M speech model + 15.3M audio decoder adapted from Kyutai's Mimi codec), two voices (Juno default, Mira), the whole model a **40.6 MB** download, running on **one CPU thread with nothing but numpy**, 24 kHz mono output, deterministic under a seed. Its specialisation is the *hard-case register*: a rule-based normaliser (standard library only) rewrites numbers, dates, times, money, codes and symbols the way people say them, and respells hard names from a built-in lexicon. Generation is self-checking: each text piece is validated as produced (runs far too long for its text, or carries a second-plus of mid-silence → redraw, up to three more takes, best kept).

**The numbers, as claimed (card).** Word error rate on Gradium TTS Hard Cases (500 sentences): **1.42% (Mira) / 1.53% (Juno)** — against Fish Audio Drama 3 at 1.89% with a 41.5 GB download (1,022× Woof's size), Phonon 31m 2.16%, Kokoro 82M 5.17%, ElevenLabs Flash v2.5 6.63%, Qwen3-TTS 1.7B 11.99%. Speed on one Apple M5 Max thread: 13.8× real-time (Juno), 7.2× (Mira); peak memory 402 MB (Juno) / 601 MB (Mira). The LFM3-Audio-2B comparison (20.1% WER) comes from a *separate* run with different instruments (Whisper transcripts), cross-checked against Kokoro's two scores — direction strong, precision soft, and the manual files it as a comparison, not a verdict. Nothing on this card has been measured on our hardware; that gap is exactly what the running bench (below) exists to close.

**What it helps us with.**

| Puzzle | What Woof changes about the attack | First move (cost) | Measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| The show's last mile (Orin: world + camera + desk) | Voice stops being a Thor service and becomes an **on-box organ**: Casey's placement ruling puts tiny TTS on the Orin, rendering the citizen's narration *spoken at the point of presentation*, on the same box whose screen he watches. This completes the frame synthesis the performance line named — clean frame (no command text) + designed subtitles (his words only) + now his words spoken — three channels, none of them plumbing. Seed determinism is the quiet load-bearing property: the same line re-rendered for an episode cut sounds identical. | **Already running:** the Orin last-mile bench (dispatched 2026-11-09) — RTF and peak RSS on the Orin's own CPU beside the live server, per-line latency text-in → wav-ready, the world's vital signs read before/after, ~10 lines transcribed back as a directional WER read. Mira's 601 MB claimed peak is the number the bench watches | Orin-measured RTF/RSS/latency vs the card's claims; cameraman landing cadence unbroken across the bench window |
| Citizens' narration / voice layer | Cody's voice+tastes layer exists as text and declaration rules; a spoken voice at 40 MB / one thread means the voice layer can render *in the world loop's own economy* — no GPU lease, no Thor window, no per-line cloud cost. The hard-case normaliser matters here in an unexpected place: citizens speak coordinates, counts, times of day and item quantities constantly — the register consumer TTS mangles worst is the register a playing citizen lives in. | Bench (after the Orin bench): render a day of Cody's real narration lines through both voices and have the takes judged cold, as audio (bench) | Misread rate on numbers/codes in citizen lines, counted by ear/transcript; cold preference between voices |
| AIR (endless radio) | Two CPU-only voices with deterministic seeds are a starter kit for hosts that must talk forever at zero marginal cost — the constraint that killed earlier radio economics was per-hour serving cost, and a numpy runtime on one thread abolishes it. | Read: note Woof as AIR's default voice candidate in the radio line's next filing (read) | Cost per broadcast hour (≈ one thread); host-voice distinguishability cold |
| LFM3-Audio open item (Casey's) | Woof's card supplies the first *measured comparison point* our research-only item has: on hard-case speech, a 1.21 GB generalist audio model scored 20.1% WER (separate instruments) against a 40 MB specialist's claimed 1.42%. This informs the item; it does not close it — the two numbers were not taken on the same bench, and LFM3-Audio does far more than TTS. | Read: attach the comparison, with its instrument caveat, to the LFM3-Audio file (read) | — (evidence for a decision that stays Casey's) |

**What it doesn't do / where the claim is soft.** Two voices, both English, both in one register family — a cast of eight citizens cannot be voiced by Woof alone, and nothing on the card speaks to emotion range, character acting, or non-English text. Every figure is author-measured on the authors' bench with model-judged transcripts; the comparison table's "download size" column is the smallest official weight-set per model, a definitional choice that flatters tidy packagers. The self-checking redraw loop means worst-case latency is a multiple of the headline speed — the bench's p90, not the card's median, is the number the show would live with. And a claim this strong (best-in-field accuracy at 1/1000th the size) deserves our standing treatment: a too-good result is a prompt to check the instrument, and the instrument here is a bench we did not build, scored by models we did not choose.

**Licence/access — BINDS.** Underdog Non-Commercial License 1.0: free for personal, research, teaching and non-profit use **including publishing what it makes**, with credit ("Voice by Underdog Woof" in any app/product; credit in title/description of published audio). Commercial or monetised use needs a licence from Underdog (license@conway.tech). **No training, fine-tuning or distillation on Woof or its audio** — absolute. Practical consequence: episode audio rendered with Woof can be published non-commercially with credit; a monetised channel would need the commercial licence first, and Woof output must never enter a training set of ours.

### 1.5 Objaverse-clean-GLB — six thousand props, licensed and shelved

**Evidence: CARD** (HF dataset codepawl/objaverse-clean-glb, read in full on arrival, 2026-11-09). Unlike the papers, a dataset card's load-test claims are mechanically checkable and were reported as counts, not scores; the quality ranking is the packer's own score.

**What it is.** The usable slice of Objaverse 1.0: 798,759 source objects filtered down to **6,015 models** — CC0 and CC-BY licences only, fan art and brand material removed, broken files removed, ranked by a quality score (likes/views/staff picks/textures; at most 40 per author). ### 1.6 Mirage (Cactus Compute) — tiny vision, and the router as a product

**Evidence: THIN CARD + VENDOR SITE** (HF Cactus-Compute/mirage card — thin; cactuscompute.com read on arrival, 2026-11-09). There is no paper. This entry is graded **vendor-stated** throughout: what follows separates what the card lists from what nobody has published, and the entry's open questions are part of its content, not an apology for it. Added by addendum — Mirage arrived after this manual was dispatched; Casey sent it as "yet another streamlined way to model," which the filing took as exactly right: it is the morning's pattern, sold as a catalogue.

**What it is.** Mirage is a small vision model for image understanding and **structured extraction**: per its overview — scene description, object detection, OCR, segmentation, spatial relations, and "decisions" (function-calling-shaped outputs). Distribution is a pip package (`cactus-compute`) that auto-downloads engine + weights on first use (mirage.cact + mirage.safetensors + config); ~10.3k downloads last month; it sits in a collection named "Automation Foundation Models For Tiny Devices." The vendor, Cactus Compute (YC-backed; 20.8k GitHub stars; 237k downloads/month claimed — vendor figures), sells sub-30 MB foundation models for tiny devices — agents, voice and vision on consumer hardware, CPU-only — with sub-2-bit quantisation claimed "near-lossless" (the vendor's word, unverified by anyone we can cite), and **hybrid routing**: every answer carries a confidence score, and the score decides whether the request stays on-device or escalates to the cloud.

**Open questions — the facts the card does not publish, listed rather than filled.** Parameter count: not stated. Weight file size: not stated (the vendor's sub-30 MB is a *line-level* claim about its foundation models, not a measured figure for Mirage). Licence: not stated on the card — for a model we might run inside a published show pipeline, this is the first question, not a detail. Benchmarks: none published — no accuracy figures for detection, OCR or extraction on any named suite. Latency: none published — no per-image time on any named device. Training data and provenance: not stated. Each of these is answerable in an afternoon (download the package, weigh the files, read the licence file, run a frame set) — which is precisely the bench this entry points at and does not perform.

## 3. Sequencing — what to attack next, and what each attack costs to enter

Two lanes are already live and are not re-litigated here: **day 9** (the show on screen: relocation, furnace day, clean frame) and **the Director** (charter + source pass + scenario #1), with the **Orin Woof bench** running beside them under yield discipline. The manual's job is the order *after* those. Recommended sequence, cheapest truth first:

1. **Finish the Woof bench and read it against §1.4's claims** — entry cost already paid (bench running). Its verdict decides whether the show gains a voice this month or the comparison shelf (Phonon/Kitten/Pocket) gets its afternoon. Either way the number that matters is Orin-measured p90 latency + Mira-voice RSS, not the card's medians.
2. **The Mirage bench, same rig, same discipline** (bench — one afternoon on the Orin, after Woof's verdict frees the box's attention). Entry cost: one package install + the licence read + a hand-labelled frame set from day-3 capture. It answers two questions for one price: does the shot log get its missing instrument, and is the vendor's "near-lossless" claim worth a second look. *Read the licence before the first frame.*
3. **The lessons-format change** (build, small — an editing pass, not a lane): Gene fields (applicability + matching signals) required on new AGENTS.md/skill entries; a probation marker for single-observation lessons; AntiGenes as a visibly separate class. Entry cost: one careful edit + a note in the doctrine. This is the highest value-per-hour item in the manual — it changes how every future day compounds, and Evolver's selected-set numbers, even discounted hard, price the direction.
4. **The Director's two read-level upgrades** (read): scenario acceptance gains (a) the harness rule — a scenario build is accepted only on independent re-execution — and (b) the AgentBaton readiness signs — artefact, feedback, localised remainder — plus (c) the scenario→Capsule→Gene learning path written into its charter. Entry cost: charter amendments. These convert three papers into standing orders for the price of prose.
5. **The Objaverse catalogue spike** (spike — when the next world-building lane opens): metadata + thumbnails to the Thor, a dozen harbour props through the engine tier into the Foundry pipeline, one portal scene dressed from the web tier. Entry cost: ~2.5 GB if the web tier is pulled whole (governor rules apply), less for a metadata-first pass. It waits because nothing live is blocked on props; it is first in its category because it is the only import whose value is *inventory* — it appreciates by sitting there, searchable.
6. **The detector spike** (spike — the most research-shaped item, deliberately last): a small local model scoring lane/citizen traces against AgentBaton's three readiness signs, compared against our current judgement calls (Tier L's graduations, Muse's lane handoffs). Entry cost: a labelled trace set — which items 3–4 generate as a by-product. Sequenced last not because it is least valuable but because it is the only attack whose *training data is our own record*, and the record gets better every week the cheaper items run.

What the manual recommends *against*, for the record: re-implementing Video-Coder's RL (24 H100-days to buy +4 points on a benchmark that isn't ours — the harness shape and the annealing rule are the imports, both free); adopting Evolver's EvoMap network (a third-party platform carrying our experience assets is a decision of a different class than borrowing its schema); and any bulk Objaverse pull beyond the catalogue pass (the pipe doctrine stands).
