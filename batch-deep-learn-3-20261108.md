## 1. MeSS — 3D urban scenes from 2D mesh via image diffusion

**What it is.** MeSS (arXiv:2508.15169; covered by Extrapolator AI, 23 Oct 2026) is a three-stage synthesis pipeline that generates multi-view-consistent, relightable street-level 3D scenes conditioned on a 2D city mesh, for autonomous-driving simulation and virtual urban navigation. Stage one: cascaded outpainting ControlNets generate a sparse set of geometrically consistent views from mesh-projected control signals (depth, segmentation, 2D layout). Stage two: AGInpaint densifies the view grid by inpainting between sparse views, conditioning on already-generated pixels via cross-attention. Stage three: GCAlign performs a global photometric correction (exposure, white balance, colour cast) across views. The output is a 3D Gaussian Splatting representation with primitives initialised directly on the mesh surface, covariance aligned to surface normals — which is what makes relighting, material swap and style transfer possible downstream.

**The bet worth naming.** MeSS explicitly argues *against* video diffusion for geometry-conditioned synthesis: temporal attention is learned, not enforced, so video models drift from prescribed camera paths; ControlNet-style conditioning is a hard geometric constraint at every denoising step, and cross-view consistency is treated as an explicit, correctable problem (GCAlign) rather than a property the backbone must internalise. The explainer's own critical notes are honest: the three-stage cascade has a compounding error surface the paper does not fully ablate; mesh-quality sensitivity (the pipeline assumes a clean, manifold mesh) is unexplored; relighting claims lack failure-case analysis at silhouette edges and under overhangs.

**Evidence / grade: A−.** A real arXiv paper with a project page, read here via a technically competent secondary explainer that includes critical observations rather than repeating claims. The grade is capped by the read being explainer-level; the paper itself is the primary source for any future use.

**Fleet mapping.** This is the sixth item in the week's Gaussian/3D thread (after SculptForge, L3G-World, Echo-3, RacerGS, FlexiAvatar — batches 1–2), and it sharpens the thread's shape: the open ground is not capture but *consistency enforcement and memory-bounded representation*. The foundry's doctrine — seed+params ARE the asset, geometry is a regenerable cache — is the opposite bet from MeSS (generate imagery, then anchor splats to a given mesh), and the two compose rather than compete: a foundry-minted parametric district could serve as exactly the clean mesh prior MeSS assumes and real city data fails to provide. GCAlign's pattern — an explicit, cheap, post-hoc coherence pass with a named failure class (photometric drift) — is the same shape as the curator's correlation fix and the league's digest reconciliation: correct in a separate pass, against a named error, rather than asking the generator to be right.

**Verdict.** Filed as the Gaussian thread's consistency reference. No spike; the foundry's authored-asset doctrine remains the stronger position for our own work.

## 2. Microsoft AgenSH — self-organised coding agents, open-sourced

**What it is.** AgenSH (the NeoTeo headline's "1,024-agent result" is one task's figure, not a benchmark mean — the article itself corrects its own headline, which is to its credit). Microsoft open-sourced AgenSH, its experiment launch scripts and a reproduction guide under MIT on 24 Oct 2026, alongside an arXiv paper. AgenSH is a harness that coordinates coding agents *without a central orchestrator*: workers gather context, **claim subtasks**, act, verify results, and merge progress asynchronously. The shared substrate is deliberately boring: **Gitea as the shared workspace, Mattermost as the messaging interface, and shared context adapted from DeLM** to retain findings and work intentions.

**The numbers, precisely.** On five ProgramBench software-reconstruction tasks (GPT-5.6-sol, high reasoning, six-hour budget, no internet), the mean final test-pass rate rose from 19.31% with one agent to 28.78% with 128 agents. The 1,024-agent figure is a *separate single-task result on Pandoc*: 33.89% (1 agent) → 55.06% (1,024 agents). Microsoft's repo separately reports GPT-6.1-sol on Pandoc: 56.19% → 79.59% at 32 agents. The release includes a single-node Docker Compose setup for an agent team and a standalone single-agent baseline.

**Evidence / grade: A−.** Paper + MIT code + reproduction guide; the results are the authors' own runs (normal for a systems paper), and the secondary coverage correctly separates the conflated figures. Nothing here ranks AgenSH against other harnesses, and the article says so.

**Fleet mapping — the strongest in this batch.** AgenSH is the parallel-lane pattern at published scale, and its mechanism is ours: workers *claiming* subtasks through a shared workspace is the mechanic our `parallel_lane_shared_state_claim` skill codifies after the Geist death (claim the seam before dispatching; one named owner per seam; reconcile by reading the live location). Three differences worth learning from. (1) **Verification is inside their loop** — workers verify results and merge progress as part of the claim cycle, not as a separate grading lane; our lanes still lean on the parent for verdicts. (2) **Their coordination substrate is off-the-shelf** (Gitea + Mattermost + a context store) — evidence that the ledger/workspace layer commoditises; what differentiates is the claim discipline and the verdict standard, which is where our doctrine already lives. (3) **The scaling curve is honest about shape**: 128 agents bought +9.5 points of mean on reconstruction tasks; 1,024 agents were spent on *one* task to buy +21 points. Parallelism pays, with steeply diminishing slope, on tasks that decompose and verify — exactly the boundary our own fork-don't-block doctrine assumes. AgenSH is also a data point for the synthesis of batches 1–2: the harness, not the model, is the product surface (Aura, Octopus, Harness² §9, and now Microsoft's entry).

**Verdict.** Read the claim/verify/merge protocol against our shared-state claim skill before the next large parallel dispatch. Shortlist item #2.

## 3. Block's Dessa — a conversation model trained only on speech

**What it is.** Dessa (block.xyz/dessa) is a full-duplex speech-to-speech model from a small AI research lab inside Block, founded by former members of Dessa, the Toronto deep-learning startup Block acquired; the lab reports directly to Jack Dorsey. The claim is categorical: Dessa was trained **from scratch on nothing but human conversation — no text, no pretrained models** — and learned language, turn-taking, backchanneling, laughter timing, emphasis and emotion purely from sound. It runs faster than real time, locally on a MacBook Pro M4 Max or better, and was trained on a single NVIDIA GB200 NVL72. The page's demos are live recordings: Dessa laughing mid-sentence rather than on cue, finishing a speaker's sentence when they blank on a movie title, gasping, modulating volume on request — none of it, the lab says, explicitly taught. Access is currently a small early-access group of AI safety researchers; no weights, no paper, no benchmark table.

**Evidence / grade: C+.** Public live recordings are real artefacts of a kind (the clips are inspectable behaviour, not renderings), and the lab's framing questions — sample efficiency of speech-only learning, in-context learning from sound — are genuinely interesting. But every capability claim rests on curated demos; there is no independent evaluation, no artefact to run, and the sample-efficiency boast ("hundreds of times fewer words" than LLMs) is unfalsifiable as stated. Demo-grade, honestly labelled by its own access model.

**Fleet mapping.** Dessa is the far pole of the voice thread that runs through this week's reading (Apriel-Voice's two-stage graft, VoxLock, Audio8, VoicePipe, ESP32-P4 ASR): every other system composes speech from text pipelines; Dessa tests whether the text bottleneck itself is the defect — the prosody, timing and affect that our radio-room dressing layer (bandpass, squelch, static) fakes *after* synthesis are, for Dessa, the native medium. For telltape/presence the mapping is doctrinal rather than practical: our presence design already refuses ambient listening and treats voice as a deliberately opened session; Dessa's safety framing ("AI is nearly deaf to the emotion in our voices" — a person in pain, unheard) is an argument *for* prosody-sensitivity inside an opened session, not for widening the session. Nothing to run, nothing to download; the item's value is as the thread's existence proof that speech-native models are being built by serious labs.

**Verdict.** Parked as a horizon marker for the voice thread. Revisit if weights or a paper appear.

## 4. NVIDIA Bolt E4B — a 4 GB talking-agent model (Nemotron 3.5)

**What it is.** Bolt E4B, released 23 Oct 2026, is NVIDIA's **Nemotron 3.5 Bolt E4B 4GB**: an open small language model built on Google's Gemma 4 E4B with NVIDIA post-training, designed for conversational agents that talk *and* call tools across turns. ~4 GB footprint, 64K context, 35 languages, switchable talk mode (short, speech-ready replies) and thinking mode. **Evidence / grade: B.** Open weights plus a vendor benchmark table that discloses its own losses — the numbers are NVIDIA's, measured as NVIDIA states, and the aggregator says exactly that. No independent runs yet.

**Fleet mapping.** Directly on our hardware line: a 4 GB conversational agent that runs at 15.5 tok/s on an Orin Nano-class board is a candidate *front-end* for the front room — the Orin hosts presence/display work, and a local talker with disciplined tool-calling (When2Call is precisely the "don't act when you should ask" judgement) fits the presence shape better than a general chat model. The honest mapping is: named candidate for a presence/radio-room front-end evaluation, benchmarked on our own twenty real exchanges, per the Lead Lab's own closing advice.

**Verdict.** Evaluation candidate, not an adoption. Shortlist item #3 (shared with the d3-ultra A/B discipline: sealed held-out, or it doesn't count).

## 8. BlenderWorld — frontier agents fail fine-grained 3D spatial coherence

**What it is.** BlenderWorld (arXiv:2610.23986, submitted 14 Oct 2026; Zhejiang University, Alibaba Token Hub, CUHK; code at github.com/QwenLM/BlenderWorld, project page blenderworld.github.io) is the first comprehensive benchmark for visual agents doing realistic 3D creation in Blender: 223 tasks across 9 categories / 22 subcategories (architecture, products, natural environments, scientific visualisation, character animation…), driven by text, image, video and structured-spec inputs. The evaluation protocol is the interesting half: submitted .blend projects are rendered from 8 agent-selected task-conditioned views and scored against **2,651 task-specific binary rubrics**, combining a **VLM-as-Judge** (visual/spatial properties from renders) with an **Agent-as-Judge** that *programmatically inspects the scene structure itself* — explicitly to bypass visual shortcuts — plus 8 visual-quality dimensions fused into one score. Thirteen frontier models were run, at 44–194 tool calls per task.

**The results.** GPT-6-Astra leads at 79.9 fused, then Opus-5 (73.0), GPT-5.6-Sol (67.7) — but even Astra fully satisfies all rubrics on only **42.2%** of tasks. The named failure modes: **viewpoint inconsistency** (geometry that looks right from the reference view and wrong from others), **view-dependent geometry shortcuts** (2D cheats instead of 3D structure), **missing ground contact / physical-constraint violations**, anatomical distortion, and a flat relation between tool calls and quality — more iteration does not fix spatial wrongness. The authors' summary: models "profoundly struggle to translate passive visual feedback into structurally sound 3D corrections."

**Evidence / grade: A−.** Paper + public code + a defined rubric artefact; the runs are the authors' (standard for a benchmark paper) and the protocol is inspectable enough to reuse.

**Fleet mapping — the highest-relevance item in the batch for the league.** Three bearings, in order of sharpness.

1. **It is the JEV spike's result, replicated by strangers at frontier scale, in the adjacent domain.** Our spike found JEPA embeddings of play footage measured the filming apparatus, not the citizen — visual representation did not carry the spatial facts. BlenderWorld finds frontier *agents* producing geometry that photographs correctly and is structurally wrong — visual plausibility does not carry spatial fact either. Both point the same way: **spatial truth lives in the state/scene representation, not in the rendered view.** The league already acts on this (verdicts from server state and scans, never from screenshots); BlenderWorld is the external citation for why that doctrine is right.
2. **Its judging split is our dock doctrine, formalised.** VLM-as-Judge grades the render; Agent-as-Judge inspects the programmatic scene — and the paper needs the second judge precisely because the first is gameable by view-dependent shortcuts. That is "a verdict can't be signed from the same store the citizen's stack writes to," and the Watcher's Charter's independent-store rule, wearing benchmark clothes. The foundry's verification should copy the shape explicitly: programmatic scene inspection (predicates over the Blender scene graph — our ensured predicates already do this) *before* any render judging, with renders graded last and least.
3. **The failure taxonomy is a checklist we can steal verbatim** for the foundry brain's builds: viewpoint inconsistency, ground contact, contact constraints, view-dependent shortcuts. Our brain's known defects (the intermittent black sail — a winding/normal error visible only from some angles — is a viewpoint-consistency failure in miniature) sit inside this taxonomy. Adopting the four named failure classes as the foundry's pre-acceptance audit costs nothing and names what we already chase by hand.

**Verdict.** Shortlist item #1: adopt the failure taxonomy + judge-split as the foundry's verification shape; cite as the external evidence for the league's state-over-screenshots doctrine.

## 9. Google Harness² — recursive agent harnessing

**What it is.** Harness² (Google Cloud AI Research + Northwestern; MarkTechPost coverage 18 Oct 2026; arXiv paper, Apache-2.0 repo, v0.1.0, project page harness2.github.io) is a framework that improves the *harness* around a frozen model — the model never changes; 0 parameters are trained. The harness is decomposed into **8 editable components, each mounted as a file**: system prompt, guardrails, memory, tool guidance, subagents, skills, scripts, plugins/middleware. Improvement runs in two linked recursions. **Task-level**: run the task with the base harness; an editor proposes two candidate harness edits (an *invariant* candidate targeting general failures — planning, tool use — and a *domain* candidate targeting knowledge/procedures); both run on the same task; a composer compares trajectories against the reference and keeps only edits backed by observed behaviour. **Harnessing-level**: after each task, a reflector stores the adopted harness plus an `EXPERIENCE.md` lessons file; the library guides future proposals, though each new task still starts from the base harness. The target setting is the open world — *no verifier, no labels* — which is the stated break from self-improvement methods that assume test cases.

**The numbers.** One refinement step (k=1) improved all 4 model×harness setups on all 3 benchmarks (WorkBuddy-Bench, JobBench, Harvey LAB; executors Gemini 3.5 Flash and GLM-5 under OpenCode and Codex; editor Claude Opus 4.8; means over 3 runs). Best: GLM-5/OpenCode on WorkBuddy 64.2 → 75.7 (+11.5). Weakest: GLM-5/Codex on JobBench +1.2. Parallel recursion at k=5 lifted Gemini/OpenCode to 80.8 on WorkBuddy. Weaker executors gained more — GLM-5 after one step beat base Gemini on WorkBuddy. In 3 of 4 setups the refined harness also used *fewer* steps per task.

**Evidence / grade: A−.** Paper + Apache-2.0 code + an honest comparison table against Meta-Harness, ReasoningBank and Harness Scaling (with the caveat, printed in the coverage, that rival scores are as measured in the Harness² paper). Early (v0.1.0) and Vertex-AI-coupled for model runs.

**Fleet mapping.** Harness² is the third independent arrival, in eight days of reading, at the same thesis: **the improvable artefact is the harness, not the model** (Aura's self-improving playtester, batch 1 §14; Octopus SI OS's self-editing runtime, study in flight; AgenSH §2). Its specific convergences with our practice are almost uncomfortable: harness components *as files* (our AGENTS.md/skills/memory layout is this, hand-maintained); an `EXPERIENCE.md` lessons library (our AGENTS.md "Lessons" section and the triples library are this, for two different substrates); contrastive keep-only-what-observed-behaviour-supports editing (our adoption rule for triples: a pass is the default plan, a fail is a fence — verdicts from world evidence). The divergence is equally instructive: Harness² edits the harness *per task, at deployment, without a verifier*, trusting trajectory contrast as the signal. Our doctrine would call that an unguarded self-modification loop — the fleet's version requires world-evidence verdicts and, for anything persistent, Casey's gate (Jacquard's adoption classification: FIX-class needs sealed held-out evidence; CHOICE-class is the owner's). Harness²'s own numbers quietly support the caution: gains are largest for the weakest executor and shrink toward noise (+1.2) exactly where the base harness is already strong — self-editing buys the most where the least has been hand-built. Cross-reference, per brief: the Octopus comparison study (in flight, ProArt ZCode) covers the self-editing-runtime angle in depth; this section deliberately does not duplicate it.

**Verdict.** The harness-thread's reference implementation for *file-mounted, contrast-edited* harnesses. The piece to steal is the invariant/domain candidate split; the piece to refuse is verifier-free adoption. Filed accordingly.

## 10. TechCrunch: "Jev is Vercel's fastest-adopted AI model ever" — the Jev disambiguation

**What the article says.** TechCrunch (Russell Brandom, 16 Oct 2026), reporting Vercel's own published AI Gateway data: within two weeks of launch, Jev was used by **35% of paid teams** on Vercel's AI Gateway — nearly double the reach of the second most popular model (Sonnet 5.5), the fastest first-two-weeks adoption Vercel has recorded. Jev launched in limited release on **15 September 2026**; it is "designed as a lightweight tool for choosing between a few predetermined outputs," running cheaper and faster than generative alternatives for classifying large quantities of data. Vercel itself adopted it early, replacing GPT-6 Luna for classifying harmful commands. The article names the creator in plain text: **"a major accomplishment for the model's creators, TypeSafe AI."** It also carries its own caveat: gateway data is a limited view, at risk of undercounting labs with their own APIs. Context figures: open-source models now account for >70% of tokens through the gateway and ~25% of spend.

**The disambiguation — the question Casey actually asked.** Is this Jev *our* Jev? Three things carry similar names in our world, and the study separates them with evidence:

1. **TypeSafe AI's Jev (the product).** A "System One" *decision model*: you send it `state` + typed `questions` (Choice / Score / Noul primitives); it returns typed answers with calibrated probability distributions — no generated text, no autoregressive decoding, 70–500 ms per decision, priced at $0.042 per million input tokens with output free (Vercel AI Gateway model ID `typesafe-ai/jev`, launched there 16 Sep 2026; TypeSafe's own launch 15 Sep 2026). Trained with what TypeSafe calls Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD). Its published limitation notes (Jev 1.13, reviewed Sep 2026) concede weakness at arithmetic, counting, date comparison and indirect reasoning. Corroboration is thick and independent: Vercel's changelog (2 Sep 2026, "TypeSafe AI: Now Available on Vercel AI Gateway"), Leonis Capital's "Jev and the Rise of Decision Models" (24 Sep 2026), DataCamp/LangChain/MindStudio explainers, and a working integration literature (AI SDK `experimental_decide` / `experimental_evaluate`). **The TechCrunch article is about this product** — it says so itself (TypeSafe AI, decision model, predetermined outputs, 15 Sep limited release), and every checkable detail matches the product's own record.
2. **Our Jacquard design's "Jev" — the same product.** Casey's architecture names "Typesafe.ai/Jev" explicitly: decision models sit behind a provider interface; Jev is advisor readings only, NEVER the gate. Batch 1 found the same product inside a shipping system (Aura Autopilot's decision model is Jev via the TypeSafe SDK — frame/triage/reflexes), and batch 2 found vLLM's d3-ultra benchmarking itself head-to-head against "Jev 1.14" (SR-Text 87.2 vs 73.1, on the vendor's private sets). All three references — ours, Aura's, d3-ultra's baseline — are the TypeSafe product. There is one Jev in this story, and it is ours.

## 11. JiRack (CMS Manhattan) — three ternary model families; and the JEV-rack question

**What it is.** LinkedIn was *not* login-walled for this read — the full Pulse article by Konstantin Grabko (CMS Manhattan), published 8 Nov 2026, was fetched complete. CMS Manhattan builds three LLM families on one foundation: **ternary weights in {−1, 0, +1}** (1.58-bit, BitLinear-style), trained ternary from scratch rather than post-hoc quantised (the article explicitly follows Microsoft BitNet's core idea with a claimed improved training pipeline; a USPTO patent application, no. 19/446,339 as spelled out in the post, is claimed filed Jan 2026 — unverified here).
