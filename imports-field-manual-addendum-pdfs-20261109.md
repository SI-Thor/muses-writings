### A.1 Foundations of Large Language Models — the fleet's shared textbook

**Evidence: FULL-TEXT** (arXiv:2501.09223v4, 8 Oct 2026; Tong Xiao & Jingbo Zhu, NLP Lab, Northeastern University & NiuTrans Research; ~280 pages + bibliography + index). **Licence: CC BY-NC 4.0** (printed on the copyright page) — a selection of chapters from the authors' open NLPBook (github.com/NiuTrans/NLPBook). **Identification: verified against its own text** — title, authors, affiliation, arXiv stamp and chapter list all match the parent's first-pass identification exactly.

**What it is.** A textbook, and the entry is graded as one. Six chapters: **1 Pre-training** (self-supervised tasks, BERT as the worked example, applications), **2 Generative Models** (the LLM build process, training at scale, long-sequence modelling), **3 Prompting** (general prompt design, advanced methods — chain-of-thought, self-consistency, least-to-most — and learning-to-prompt: soft prompts, automatic prompt design), **4 Alignment** (instruction fine-tuning, RLHF, improved preference alignment incl. DPO-class methods and the overoptimisation problem), **5 Inference** (prefilling and decoding — greedy, beam, nucleus sampling; efficient inference — KV cache, speculative execution, iteration-level scheduling; inference-time scaling: sequential and parallel), **6 Reasoning** (reasoning via prompting, inference-time reasoning, learning to reason — on/off-policy distillation, RL — and evaluating reasoning models). The preface states the intent verbatim: the book "focuses more on the foundational aspects of large language models rather than providing comprehensive coverage of all cutting-edge methods," with chapters written to be self-contained.

**The mechanism — what a textbook's mechanism is.** There is no result here to replicate; the mechanism is *settled pedagogy with worked formalism*. Each chapter builds the concept from its definition (a prompt is the input text x, output y drawn from Pr(y|x); a template is text with placeholders) through the standard algorithms to a summary, with exercises in the NLPBook tradition. Its authority is the field's consensus as of its writing, curated by a working NLP lab — which is precisely a different, and in some ways stronger, kind of authority than any single paper in the manual: nothing in it depends on one bench, one lab's harness, or one selected diagnostic set. The price is symmetric: nothing in it is *news*. Its frontier is the field's frontier minus the writing lag — citations run through 2024–2026, so the lag is short, but the reasoning chapter's settled core (CoT, self-consistency, inference-time scaling, distillation) is the 2023–2025 consensus, not this month's.

**What it helps us with.** A reference entry maps differently from a result entry: not "this changes the attack" but "this chapter is the citation and the shared vocabulary for a procedure we already run." **What it doesn't do / where the claim is soft.** A textbook cannot be *wrong* the way a paper can, but it can be *behind*, and three limits bind. ### A.2 Stochastic engrams — a gate on what may harden, in a network that fits in 3 MB

**Evidence: FULL-TEXT** (Aguilar, Herbozo Contreras & Kavehei, School of Biomedical Engineering, University of Sydney; *APL Machine Learning* 4, 046108 (2026); submitted 4 Jun 2026, accepted 26 Oct 2026, published online 6 Nov 2026; DOI 10.1063/5.0346731). **Licence: CC BY 4.0** (stated on the article). **Identification: verified** — title, authors, journal, article number and mechanism all match the first-pass identification; two of its claims require the corrections filed in the closing section of this entry, below.

**What it is.** A continual-learning architecture, **engramBNN**, built for the catastrophic-forgetting problem on resource-constrained edge devices: a *binarized* neural network (weights and activations in {−1, +1}, trained through real-valued "hidden weights") whose hidden layers are modulated per-input by a learned stochastic gate — the engram — while a metaplasticity rule governs how hard each synapse is to change. It is the only strategy in its comparison set to average above 70% on both an MNIST class-incremental and an MNIST domain-incremental benchmark, at 3.02 MiB of inference memory — and the distance between that sentence and anything we run is most of this entry.

**The mechanism, precisely (full-text, §§II–III).** Three components, and the entry keeps them separate because the mapping below uses them separately.

1. **The engram gate.** In the biology the authors cite, an engram is a sparse ensemble of neurons tagged by an experience, whose selective reactivation *is* recall, gated by context. Their construction: a single shared, full-precision, two-layer encoder (input → 512 → 128 → 64) compresses each input into a latent code — explicitly *not* an autoencoder; the code exists to drive modulation, not reconstruction. Per hidden layer of the backbone, a linear projection (64 → 2048) produces gating logits; a sigmoid turns them into probabilities; a **top-k** operation keeps only the k largest (k chosen on validation; k = 409 of 2048 = **20% of units retained, 80% sparsity**); during training the mask is then **sampled**, M ∼ Bernoulli(p̃), element-wise, so the gate is genuinely stochastic while learning; gradients flow only through active units, via a straight-through estimator. At inference the sampling is switched off — deterministic top-k — "to efficiently evaluate the exact engram assigned to a given input." The mask multiplies the hidden activations element-wise, so each input is routed through a sparse, context-chosen subnetwork. The paper insists on two contrasts, and both matter for the mapping: unlike **dropout**, the gate is active at test time and is *context-driven* (dropout's masks are context-free noise, training-only); unlike **XdG/WSN**, the gate needs **no task identity** — context is inferred from the input itself, per sample, on every forward pass.
2. **Metaplasticity.** Each binary synapse's sign is the visible state; behind it a real-valued hidden weight W_h whose magnitude is its consolidation. The update rule (Algorithm 1; f_meta(m, W_h) = 1 − tanh²(m·W_h), from Laborieux et al.) scales any gradient step that would *shrink* |W_h| by f_meta: the larger |W_h| already is, the more the shrink is suppressed, so a consolidated synapse's sign becomes progressively harder to flip, while growth is unimpeded. The hyperparameter m is the stability–plasticity dial, set per dataset (m = 5 Split MNIST; 250 Split CIFAR-100; 175 CORe50), and the paper is explicit about the trade: raise m and old tasks hold better while new-task peak performance falls. Nothing is ever frozen by decree; consolidation is a *rate*, continuously priced.
3. **The binarized backbone.** Two hidden layers of 2048 units for the MLP experiments (batch-norm statistics isolated per task — saved at task end, reloaded for that task's inference, an easily missed detail: the *normalisation state* is part of what is remembered); a binarized ResNet-18 for CIFAR-100, where the gate is broadcast spatially over convolutional feature maps. Training: 20 epochs per task, Adam at 10⁻⁴, and — filed here because §IV is easy to misread — **a 2000-sample reservoir replay buffer, mixed 1:1 with current-task data, given to engramBNN and to every baseline alike.**

**The numbers, with their fine print attached (full-text, Tables I–VII; all author-measured, six runs, mean ± sd).**

- **Class-IL Split MNIST (5 tasks × 2 classes, single head, no task ID): engramBNN ACC 72.65 ± 1.42%, wACC 76.31%** — against vanilla BNN 15.83%, mBNN (metaplasticity alone) 29.94%, EWC 20.05%, LwF 20.12%, XdG 19.74%, SI 9.76%, SDMLP 14.47%, CH-HNN 12.08%, WSN 8.94%. Its retention is *not* the best in the table: BWT −33.10 (it still forgets a third of its early performance), and SI's REM is 99.78 against engramBNN's 66.90 — SI retains nearly everything by learning almost nothing (ACC 9.76%, near chance). The honest reading is the paper's own: engramBNN is the only entry that *both* learns new tasks *and* keeps most of the old.
- **Domain-IL Split MNIST (odd/even across tasks): engramBNN ACC 75.34%, wACC 79.47%** — best ACC and wACC; vanilla BNN took raw ACC on CORe50-NI (20.94% vs 17.88%) while engramBNN took wACC (19.06%) and FWT (16.49%) there. **CORe50-NI is the paper's reality check: every method sits at 10–21% ACC** on the realistic dataset; engramBNN leads the weighted metric, it does not conquer the benchmark.
- **Class-IL Split CIFAR-100 (binarized ResNet-18, replay buffer = 4% of training data): engramBNN is best on both splits — ACC 8.12% (20–5) and 9.66% (10–10)** vs vanilla BNN 4.05/5.90 — a genuine 2× relative gain at an absolute level the paper itself calls near-collapse for the whole field ("almost all models exhibited final accuracies near or only marginally above random chance" — 100-class chance is 1%, so 8–10% is 8–10× chance, and still unusable as a classifier). Several baselines' ~100% REM scores here are plasticity collapse, named as such by the authors.
- **Permuted MNIST (Table VII, appendix): engramBNN 52.94% ACC — NOT the best**; SDMLP (63.27%) and XdG (56.98%) beat it. The authors state in the discussion that they consider Permuted MNIST an ineffective benchmark, included for its prevalence; the table stands regardless, and any citation of this paper as "best everywhere" is false.
- **Ablation (Class-IL Split MNIST, Table IV): the gate is the result.** engramBNN 72.06% at 0.80 sparsity with average mask stability 0.70; mBNN alone 30.20%; BNN + dropout 20.94%. Metaplasticity without gating buys ~14 points over vanilla; gating on top buys ~42 more. Representation analyses agree (silhouette 0.24 vs vanilla 0.10; RDM off-diagonal dissimilarity 0.96 vs 0.86).
- **Resources (Tables V–VI):** peak GPU utilisation 4.51% (of a ~17 GB card, WandB-polled during *training*, PyTorch, no inference engine) — lowest in the set, against EWC's 92.74%; peak RAM 19.51% (not the lowest: WSN 2.63%, SI 6.66%); **inference memory 3.02 MiB** for 6.43M parameters, vs 24.53 MiB for the identical architecture in full precision (the ~8× figure is *this ratio*), 26.30 MiB BI-Replay, 430.97 MiB PNN — and vs **0.69 MiB for the plain mBNN**: the engram block's ~600k extra parameters (the full-precision encoder) make engramBNN ~4× *heavier* than its own backbone. The gate is not free; it is cheap relative to full precision and expensive relative to the ungated net.

**What it doesn't do / where the claim is soft.** (1) **Scale**: the strongest results are MNIST — greyscale digits, fully connected nets, five tasks. The moment the data gets realistic, the paper's own tables sober up: CORe50-NI ACC ≤ ~21% for everyone; CIFAR-100 ACC 8–10% for the winner. This is a mechanism demonstration, not a capability result, and any use of "matches full-precision SOTA" must carry its scope (below). (2) **The replay confound**: engramBNN trains *with* a 2000-sample replay buffer, as do all baselines — fair inside the paper, but the abstract's framing ("integrates stochastically activated engrams as a gating mechanism") invites a no-replay reading the experiments don't support; the gate's isolated contribution is the ablation's (72.06 vs 30.20), measured *with* replay held constant. (3) **Hand-set dials**: m per dataset (5/250/175 — a 50× spread) and k (20% retention) are validation-chosen per benchmark; nothing learns them, so the valve's setting is currently an experimenter's judgement, the very thing our graduation doctrine tries to remove from the loop. (4) **The batch-norm side-channel**: per-task BN statistics are stored and reloaded — a small, legitimate, *task-indexed* memory sitting inside a "task-agnostic" claim; at inference the correct task's statistics must be selected, which quietly reintroduces a task signal the headline doesn't mention. (5) **Resource figures are training-machine percentages** (WandB on a ~17 GB GPU), not edge-device measurements — the authors themselves defer on-device inference benchmarking to future work; "under 5% GPU" is a fraction of a desktop card, not a wattage. (6) Single paper, one lab, no independent replication; the backbone (Laborieux-style mBNN) is the authors' own prior line, so the comparison set's centre of gravity is friendly.

The first-pass note repeated the abstract's scope; the tables' scope is narrower and stands. (ii) The first-pass "<5% peak GPU, <20% RAM" reads as dominance; Table V shows the RAM figure (19.51%) is mid-pack (WSN 2.63%, SI 6.66% are lower) and the GPU figure is a percentage of a ~17 GB training card. ### A.3 Proving and Improving the Platonic Representation Hypothesis with JEPAs — the good-views condition

**Evidence: FULL-TEXT** (Balestriero & LeCun, Brown / NYU / AMI Labs; arXiv:2610.39302v1, 29 Oct 2026; theory §§2/A, experiments §§3–5/C, limitations §7 — all read). **Identification: verified** — title, authors, arXiv stamp and claims match the first-pass identification; the first-pass numbers all survive the full text, with two precisions filed at this entry's tail.

**What it is.** A paper that converts the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al.: models trained on different modalities converge toward one representation of reality as they scale) from an observation into a *conditional theorem* for Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures, identifies exactly which condition does the work — the **positive pairs** — and then validates the condition empirically from 145k captions to the texts of 940M web images, including a scaling law for alignment itself. For this fleet it is the most consequential of the three PDFs and the one requiring the most care, because it speaks directly to our closed JEPA lines; §"What it does not change" below is part of the entry, not a footnote to it.

**The mechanism — the theorem and its conditions, stated at the strength the paper states them (full-text, §2, §7).** The setup is deliberately idealised, and the paper says so in its own conclusion: a **finite** world of states z; each modality renders a state **injectively** (an observation determines its state); all modalities share the same marginal over states; training is taken to its **population optimum**. A JEPA here is stripped to its spectral skeleton — encoder minimising a prediction loss between the embeddings of a positive pair plus an anti-collapse term — with the **predictor set to the identity** ("as in our experiments… whereas JEPAs used as world models predict one embedding from the other through a learned predictor, often conditioned on an action, **which our analysis does not cover**").

- **Theorem 1 (Platonic Representation Theorem).** If M ≥ 2 modalities share *one positive-pair distribution* over world states (π₁ = … = π_M = π) and the objective's minimisers are unique up to rotation — shown for second-order (whitening) anti-collapse under a spectral gap (Proposition 1: the minima are exactly the top-k eigenvectors of the normalised positive-pair graph, min loss 2Σ(1−µᵢ)) — then every modality's optimal encoder satisfies f⋆_m ∘ g_m = Q_m h⋆ for a single world embedding h⋆ and rotations Q_m. Separately trained encoders agree, up to one rotation each. Uniqueness under LeJEPA's full-distribution SIGReg constraint is *assumed, not proved* (the paper flags this itself).
- **Theorem 2 (the bound when positives differ).** Misalignment after the best rotation is bounded by the mismatch between the two modalities' positive-pair kernels, divided by the spectral gap — and the bound is validated on 300 randomly generated finite worlds (observed error ≤ 18% of the bound in every world). Directions near the spectral gap are protected; directions at the spectrum's tail "need not align at all."
- **Good views vs bad views (Theorem 3, Proposition 3, Theorem 4).** Enrich the rendering with a per-observation **nuisance** ν (a caption's wording, a photo's viewpoint). A **good view** pairs two *independent observations* of related states — they share only the state. A **bad view** pairs two *corruptions of one observation* — they share the nuisance too. Results: a good-view JEPA learns a function of the state alone (scene-explained fraction ρ = 1) and spends **no** embedding directions on nuisance; a bad-view JEPA spends r ≥ 1 of its k directions on nuisance whenever its corruptions preserve the nuisance more than the state's k-th direction (β₁ > α_k), and those directions are unalignable by any rotation — a floor of alignment error no scale removes. A world model that *acts* through a camera must still track viewpoint; the theorem's point is narrower: no caption can determine it, so it cannot be part of the *shared* representation.
- **Proposition 4 (next-token prediction is a bad-view JEPA).** The NTP loss decomposes into a prediction term plus the softmax log-partition as its anti-collapse term; its positives are a prefix and its continuation *within one document* — which share the document's nuisance (author, wording, style). Two further weaknesses the paper is careful to mark as heuristic rather than corollary: NTP's minimisers are identified only up to an invertible linear map GL(k), not a rotation; and contexts sharing a continuation become weak implicit positives of each other, joining scenes only through long chains (hence: alignment *through scale*, slowly) while frequent tokens join contexts *across* scenes (spill). "A prediction to test rather than a corollary" — their words for the bridge from Proposition 3 to NTP.

**The empirical protocol and results (full-text, §§3–5; author-measured, typically three seeds).** All text encoders are BERT-family trained from scratch (11M / 33M / 109M / 335M) with one LeJEPA recipe (invariance + SIGReg, λ = 0.02), differing *only in their positives*; image side mostly a frozen DINOv2, so results compare directly with the PRH paper's own benchmark. Metrics: mutual 10-NN overlap (fit-free; chance .01) and retrieval R@1/R@5 through **one rotation fitted on validation pairs**.

- **Flickr30k, matched data and epochs (Table 1):** good-view (five captions of one image as views) mutual 10-NN **.062** vs bad-view (masked spans of one caption) **.047** vs NTP **.042** vs NTP-trained-on-caption-pairs **.043**; R@1 .044 / .027 / .021. Good views win every metric; bad views and NTP *nearly coincide*, as the theory predicts. Absolute levels are low (this is neighbourhood overlap, not retrieval accuracy) — the *ordering* is the result.
- **Scale (texts of 2.16M → 940M web images, Recap-DataComp; §4):** a 33M good-view JEPA on 50M-image texts reaches WIT-1024 **.125**, level with Qwen2.5-3B and -7B; the **109M good-view JEPA on 940M-image texts reaches .184 on WIT-1024, above every LM evaluated — Qwen2.5-72B (.154), Llama-3-70B in the same band — and above CLIP's text encoder (.124)**, having read 116B text tokens against their 15–18T. With CLIP ViT-H as the image side the figure is .230 vs .210. One honest asymmetry the headline skips: against from-scratch *image* JEPAs and on Flickr R@1, the ordering good > NTP > bad holds (bad falls last there); encoder size helps only to 109M (335M *drops*: .376 vs .400 on the reported column).
- **The 42× (§4, Figure 1):** on the same corpus, the good-view JEPA reaches with **21.6M samples** the WIT alignment NTP reaches with **900M** (.109 vs .111). Precision the first-pass note needs: 42× is in *samples*; in text tokens the paper's own figure caption says **14× fewer** (a caption-view sample carries more text). Both numbers are the paper's; cite the one you mean.
- **The scaling law (§4.4):** R@1 as a function of samples seen M and good-view fraction q, R@1 = a + d·log M + b·log(1 + q^α M^β / N^γ / c), fitted on a 45-cell grid (RMSE .007), then used to **predict 85 runs before training them: prediction RMSE .0093 vs seed-to-seed .0096** — as accurate as rerunning with another seed, up to ~2× the fitted range. The paper states its limits in the same breath: at 4× range it *overestimates* good-view runs (repeated data has no term), exponents are "poorly identified," and it is "an empirical summary of one recipe, not a consequence of Theorem 4."
- **The showcase (§5.1):** a caption decoder trained on *text alone* (reconstruct captions from frozen text-JEPA embeddings, with Gaussian noise matched to the measured alignment error, σ ≈ 3) + frozen DINOv2/DINOv3 + **one rotation fitted on 30k Flickr pairs** = a captioner that never trained on a pair: **CIDEr .838 on COCO** (consensus of 32 samples; .832 with the demo decoder), .519 on Flickr; with the rotation fitted on Flickr only it still transfers (.668 COCO). Rotations fitted on as few as 30 pairs already retrieve-level performance (.169 CIDEr vs .158 for 1,014-pair retrieval). The same captioner, fed the mean of eight frame embeddings, captions video it never trained on (MSR-VTT CIDEr .246 — above the zero-shot CLIP-based captioners, below trained video–language systems .271–.348).
- **Pair-free alignment (§5.2):** with *no pairs at all*, Schnaus et al.'s geometry-matching method applied to the 109M good-view JEPA beats every zero-shot accuracy in that benchmark's 21 model pairs (ImageNet-100 top-5 **64.4%** vs their best 56%; Qwen3-8B rerun under identical conditions: 47.6%) and matches CLIP's text encoder on both CIFARs — though CLIP's text encoder, trained on 400M pairs, still wins FOSCTTM (.006 vs .018) and ImageNet-100 (68.7% vs 64.4%). On smaller unpaired pools (Flickr halves), *only* good-view JEPAs leave chance (R@1 16.4% vs ≤0.3% for everything else incl. CLIP's text encoder).

**What it helps us with — and what it does not change.** The mapping is deliberately written in two halves. The first half is the design input the charter commissioned. The second half is the guard-rail, verified against both this paper and our filed verdicts before writing.

**What it does NOT change — the closed lines stay closed, and the paper itself is the reason.** Our two JEPA negatives were: (1) **LA-JEPA (sealed verdict)**: at ~2% of the paper's training budget, on a photometric-only OOD proxy, the future-prediction loss helped modestly while *representation-space prediction lost ~8.6× to an explicit conv VAE* — a readout result about what the learned features were good *for*, on our bench. (2) **The observer cut (confound verdict)**: I-JEPA/V-JEPA embeddings over citizen footage separated *locations and eras* at least as well as citizens — the stream measured the filming apparatus first; the placebo dissolved the behaviour claim. Read against Balestriero & LeCun, both negatives are exactly what the good-views condition *predicts* for our setups, not exceptions to it: our footage views were crops and clips of a single observation stream (bad views, sharing the camera's nuisance), our placebos *were* the nuisance winning, and the theorem's floor (Theorem 3) says no readout recovers dimensions spent on that nuisance. **The paper therefore explains our negatives; it does not overturn them, and it does not reopen either line.** What it changes is only the design space of any *future* perception question: such a question is now chartered, if ever, as a good-views construction (paired independent observations, rotation-tested readout, placebo by construction) — a different experiment from both closed ones, requiring its own commission from Casey, not a rematch smuggled in under this paper's authority. Filed here so the next session doesn't have to re-derive the boundary.

**What it doesn't do / where the claim is soft.** (1) **The theorem is not about the JEPAs anyone deploys**: finite world, injective renderings, matched marginals, population optima, identity predictor, second-order anti-collapse — the authors enumerate these themselves ("no experiment meets the hypotheses of Theorem 1 exactly"; real modalities are *lossy*, and lossy observations "remain open"). Action-conditioned world-model JEPAs — the kind our lines used — are explicitly outside the analysis. (2) **The good views at scale are partly distilled**: on Recap-DataComp, two of each image's three texts are LLaVA recaptions — generated by a model whose vision encoder is CLIP, i.e. by image–text training. The paper discloses this prominently and quantifies the captioner-free version (alt-text + paraphrase: WIT .060 vs .107) — so "paired information enters through the definition of positives" is honest, but the *scale* comparisons against LMs and CLIP ride partly on a captioner's paired past; the *between-views* comparisons on identical texts are clean. (3) **Hyperparameters were tuned only for the good-view JEPA** (authors' admission) — the bad-view and NTP arms may be under-tuned, which matters most exactly where margins are thinnest (Flickr). (4) **Absolute alignment remains modest**: mutual 10-NN .184 at best, post-rotation cosines ≈ .3 — the captioner works *despite* weak alignment because its decoder is noise-matched to it; "the same representation" is a statement about neighbourhood structure up to rotation, not about interchangeable embeddings. (5) The image side is *mostly* a frozen bad-view encoder (DINOv2 — crops of one photo, by the paper's own definition), so the flagship comparisons are good-text vs bad-image; fully good-view image training is, by their account, future work with weak early results. (6) v1 preprint, one lab, unreplicated; the scaling law's exponents are poorly identified by the authors' own statement.

**Precisions to the morning's first-pass note.** (i) "42× fewer samples" stands, in samples; in tokens it is 14× (the paper's Figure 1) — cite the unit. (ii) The rotation-captioner figure (CIDEr .838 COCO) is the *best* configuration (DINOv3 image side, text-only decoder, consensus-of-32 decoding, rotation on 30k pairs); the same construction scores .519 on Flickr and .396–.431 in its plainest form — the showcase number is real and is a maximum, not a default. Neither precision reverses the first pass; both belong on the record.
